About this project

What does it mean to say an AI "understands" a broken sentence?

AIphasia is a formal ontology and knowledge graph mapping 40 artificial intelligence tools built for aphasia, together with a statistical meta-analysis that asks a more specific question about how they work.

Aphasia, briefly

Aphasia is a language disorder caused by brain damage, most often a stroke. It can affect speaking, understanding, reading, or writing, without touching intelligence itself. Roughly a third of stroke survivors experience it.

In the last five years a wave of AI tools has appeared, promising to diagnose it faster, predict recovery, personalise therapy, or restore communication altogether. This project catalogues that wave systematically, then checks it against the real clinical shape of the disorder.

What "meta-analysis" means here

Most reviews of a research field describe it in prose. A meta-analysis does arithmetic on it instead: it takes several separate studies that measured something comparable, converts each result into a shared unit called an effect size, and combines them into one number with a margin of error.

The question changes from "does it work?" to "by how much, on average, and how confident can we be?"

Here that method is applied to a sharper question, suggested by faculty feedback on this project: does it matter where in the process an AI system tries to understand language?

The point of this project is not to produce a longer catalogue. It is to line up two different ways of getting an AI system to engage with broken language, and to see where each one actually falls short. The two columns below are that comparison. Open each one.

Approach A

Behavioural

Reads what a person actually produces: speech, text, scores on a clinical test. Most diagnostic and therapeutic AI in this field works this way, classifying, predicting, or generating from that output, however broken it already is.

It converged fast, and that speed is also its limit. Almost two thirds of the tools mapped here do not even specify which aphasia subtype they target, and coverage concentrates on two or three convenient syndromes while entire clinical categories go untouched.

Easy to build, easy to evaluate, and for that reason easy to stop improving.

Approach B

Inner speech

Tries to reach the intended word before, or instead of, the damaged channel that would normally speak it. Decodes directly from neural signal, EEG or similar, recorded during inner or imagined speech.

Grounded in a real clinical fact: inner speech in aphasia often survives better than the ability to say it aloud.

It is young enough that no study reviewed here was run on a single person with aphasia, only healthy volunteers standing in for the population the method is meant to serve.

Sample sizes are tiny, some as small as four participants, and results swing from near chance to strong accuracy depending on setup. Promising, but not yet reliable enough to call settled.

The meta-analysis later in this project puts an exact number on that comparison. The two gaps above are the reason it was worth running in the first place.

Related work

Three reference points sit near this project, and none of them do what it does. Naming the gap between them and AIphasia is the clearest way to state its contribution.

SNOMED CT

The broadest clinical terminology in use, with hundreds of thousands of concepts and relations covering essentially every diagnosis, finding, and procedure in medicine, aphasia included.

What it doesn't do: SNOMED CT is a terminology to code against, not a reasoning system built around one clinical question. It has no notion of an AI tool, an evidence level, or a pooled effect size, and nothing in it is meant to be queried by a DL reasoner for a specific research comparison.

breadth, not inference

HPO (Human Phenotype Ontology)

A formal OWL ontology of phenotypic abnormalities, built for exactly the kind of DL reasoning AIphasia also uses, and the closest of the three in engineering style: defined classes, cross-references to other ontologies, used at scale in rare-disease diagnostics.

What it doesn't do: HPO describes phenotypes, not the tools built to detect or treat them, and it carries no evidence-synthesis layer — no effect sizes, no pooled estimates, nothing resembling a meta-analysis as a first-class graph citizen.

closest in DL style

ICF (WHO)

The classification this project actually reuses: linguistic domains and communication modalities carry explicit ICF codes (e.g. OralComprehension → b1670), read directly off an external clinical standard rather than invented locally.

What it doesn't do: ICF classifies function and disability descriptively. It isn't formalised as OWL DL, carries no automated consistency checking, and was never meant to catalogue AI tools or their supporting evidence.

reused, not duplicated

What's actually new here

No project in this set combines a formal OWL catalogue of a clinical AI field with a statistical evidence-synthesis layer inside the same graph. Cohen's h, pooled random-effects estimates, I² heterogeneity, and a publication-bias check are all individuals a SPARQL query can retrieve — not numbers sitting in a separate PDF.

ICF's clinical alignment, plus HPO's DL-grade engineering, plus a meta-analysis as a queryable part of the graph: that pairing is the niche none of the other two occupy.

State of Affairs

Aphasia is not one thing. The ontology treats it as twelve.

Before mapping any AI tool, the project had to settle a prior question: what counts as a type of aphasia, and by whose criteria.

Two branches, two causes

The taxonomy splits along etiology, the underlying cause, because it changes everything downstream: how the disorder starts, how it develops, and what an AI tool would even need to detect.

The taxonomy as a tree

Sudden, vascular onset on one side. Gradual, neurodegenerative onset on the other. Each leaf below is one of the twelve subtypes. Red means no AI tool in the graph targets it at all. Click a leaf to read the clinical literature that defines that specific syndrome.

Which approach are we actually comparing

This is the same split introduced in About, now with the real nine tools that entered the meta-analysis, so the two categories stop being abstract.

Approach A, behavioural (k=4)
SONIVA, stroke detection from Whisper embeddings↗SVM on EEG neural tracking (De Clercq et al. 2024)↗ASR plus SVM on Dutch aphasic speech (Barberis et al. 2024)↗PLORAS, outcome prediction from lesion and imaging↗

All four read a signal that already exists: recorded speech, an EEG response to heard speech, or clinical and imaging data. That makes them relatively easy to validate on large samples, and the pooled estimate is tight for that reason.

But none is built for a particular syndrome, and none tries to recover what a person meant when the output itself is what has failed.

Approach B, inner speech (k=5)
LSTM with wavelet scattering, 4 imagined commands↗EEG Transformer, 13 imagined classes↗Single channel EEG, imagined Chinese characters↗EEGNet, 5 imagined Spanish words↗CNNeeg1-1, 5 imagined vowels↗

Three of the five studies have nine participants or fewer, and every participant was a healthy volunteer, not a person with aphasia. The tasks differ wildly, from four imagined commands to thirteen classes, so accuracy swings from near chance to near ceiling.

It aims at exactly the right target, but the evidence for it working where it is needed is still missing.

None of these nine tools target one specific subtype from the tree above. They work at the level of detecting aphasia in general or estimating its severity, not at the level of individual syndromes, which is itself part of the pattern this project keeps running into.

An honest caveat, built into the ontology itself. The categorical model above is the most common in clinical practice, but it isn't the only one, and it isn't beyond dispute:

  • An alternative cognitive-neuropsychological model decomposes language into discrete processing modules rather than syndromes.
  • Critics such as Ardila (2010) argue the categorical model misses mixed profiles and ignores cognitive deficits that occur alongside language loss.

This project adopts the categorical model because it matches how nearly every AI diagnostic tool in the field is trained — a declared modelling choice, not an assumed clinical fact.

Standards alignment, and why it isn't enough

The ontology cross-references ICD-11 (MA80.0) and ICD-10-CM (R47.01), but both codify aphasia only at the general level, with no distinct codes for individual subtypes. That gap is why a dedicated, finer-grained ontology has independent descriptive value here.

From a taxonomy to a question worth testing

None of this classification work was the end goal. It was the precondition for asking a sharper question.

Once every AI tool in the field is tagged against these twelve subtypes, a pattern becomes visible that no single paper would show on its own: most tools cluster around two or three convenient syndromes, and two clearly different engineering strategies emerge for reaching the remaining ones.

One reads what a person manages to say. The other tries to reach the word before speech happens at all.The split carried forward into the rest of this project

The Ontology section shows how it is built into the graph itself. The Graphs section shows the coverage gaps in numbers. The meta-analysis compares the two strategies directly and asks whether one actually outperforms the other, or whether the field is currently too young and too uneven to tell.

Ontology

One hinge class holds the whole graph together.

An ontology is just a formal, checkable way of describing a domain: what kinds of things exist, and how they relate. Here, the two kinds of things are AI tools and clinical facts about aphasia. The diagram below shows how they connect. Click any box to see what is actually inside it.

The hinge class Defined classes & the meta-analysis The model, topic by topic Patterns & refused questions

Every AI tool links to the clinical world through one class that does all the work, ClinicalApplication. Instead of attaching simple tags directly to a tool (a subtype here, a score there), the graph creates one node per judgment, which then points outward to:

  • exactly which subtype is targeted,
  • how strong the evidence is, and
  • what the tool is known to get wrong.

Nothing is flattened into a label that could quietly go stale.

A defined class, and an honest note on how it is actually evaluated

UndifferentiatedByAphasiaSubtype is not a label someone typed in by hand. It is defined through a cardinality rule, not a manual annotation: a clinical application belongs to it if it declares no subtype.

Under OWL's open-world reading, a reasoner cannot conclude "no subtype exists" purely from "none is written down" — there could always be one not yet asserted.Why this isn't a simple reasoner query

So this graph treats its catalogue of 40 tools as deliberately closed for this one analytic purpose — a complete, curated list of everything found in the literature, not a sample — and runs the definition as a closed-world query instead.

Doing it this way, rather than quietly assuming the reasoner handles it, is what turns the gap into something you can trust, not just something you can query.

The meta-analysis, built into the graph itself

Nine of the 40 tools carry a reified measurement of their own accuracy, converted into a shared statistic called Cohen's h. Those nine feed into two pooled estimates, one per approach, which are then compared directly inside the graph.

Click through the boxes below to see exactly which nine studies, which two pools, and what the final comparison says.

The clinical taxonomy

Two etiological branches under AphasiaType, each ending in disjoint leaf syndromes. The post-stroke branch follows the neoclassical Boston model; the PPA branch follows Gorno-Tempini et al. (2011) plus the 2024 atypical category.

Symptoms and linguistic domains

manifestsSymptom and affectsDomain connect a syndrome down to the symptoms it produces and the linguistic level each symptom hits. coOccursWithSymptom is symmetric: asserting one direction lets a reasoner recover the other on its own.

Modalities, extra-linguistic domains and diagnostic tests

Each diagnostic test now links to the communication modality and the symptom it actually assesses, instead of sitting as a bare label under DiagnosticTest.

AI tools and techniques

A sample of tools and the techniques they employ, plus the communication modality each technique actually processes where that is honestly determinable from the source literature.

The ClinicalApplication hinge

The reified n-ary pattern at the centre of the graph, its qualified cardinality constraints, its alignment to DOLCE-Ultralite (dul:Situation / dul:Object / dul:Concept), and the property chain that lets a reasoner connect a tool straight to a subtype.

Evidence and limitations

The five-point evidence scale and the six orthogonal kinds of limitation, plus the two defined classes derived from them: one from an absence (no declared subtype), one from a declared value (evidence level 1 or 2).

The dataset behind the graph

Every class and property above is only as good as what actually populates it. There was no existing registry of "AI tools applied to aphasia" to import, so the 40 entries were compiled by hand from the published and vendor literature, one ClinicalApplication at a time.

Scope. An entry qualifies if it is an AI-based tool, prototype, or published study (2007–2026) that:

  • diagnoses, treats, predicts the course of, or assists communication in aphasia;
  • has a traceable public source — a paper, a preprint, a clinical trial record, or a vendor's own published evidence.

This is a curated catalog of what was found, not a systematic review with a registered protocol: a tool's absence from it means it wasn't found, not that it doesn't exist.

A lightweight pitfall audit, run directly against the .ttl file. Beyond the eight CQs, a manual OOPS!-style sweep for common OWL antipatterns (missing domain/range, hierarchy cycles, redundant subclassing, disjointness gaps) turned up a clean result:

  • Every one of the 39 declared properties carries both an rdfs:domain and an rdfs:range — no silent gaps for a reasoner to misuse.
  • The class hierarchy has no cycles and no redundant subclass assertions (no class restates both a direct and an indirect parent).
  • Disjointness is declared through three owl:AllDisjointClasses groups rather than pairwise axioms — and GlobalAphasia is deliberately left out of the post-stroke group, because it is intentionally asserted as a subclass of both FluentAphasia and NonFluentAphasia, a documented exception, not an oversight.
  • The 34 classes with no asserted instances are not dead weight: 12 are the aphasia-subtype leaves, which the ontology deliberately puns as both classes (for subsumption and disjointness) and named individuals (so they can be used as property values) — a pattern already documented inline in the .ttl file itself; 2 are the defined classes, correctly populated only by inference, never by hand; 1 is the unpopulated local DOLCE stub kept for documentary alignment.

The honest result of running this instead of just asserting it: the ontology holds up against all eight competency questions and against a direct antipattern sweep, with every class and property behaving exactly as its own documentation says it should.

Conclusions

A null result is not the same as no result.

The headline number is p = 0.796. No statistically significant difference between AI that reads behaviour and AI that reads inner speech. It would be easy to read that as a failed comparison. It found something more interesting instead.

Why the null result still matters

With only k=4 and k=5 studies per family, a non significant test has limited power. It cannot, on its own, prove the two approaches are equivalent. But it does something more specific and more useful here.

The gap shrank as more studies were added

0.975 → 0.962
Family A pooled h, k=7 then k=9
0.641 → 0.878
Family B pooled h, k=7 then k=9

That direction of movement is informative on its own: it suggests the initial gap was mostly a small sample artefact, not a stable ceiling on covert decoding.

The number that deserves more attention than the p-value

Look instead at I², the share of variation between studies that isn't just sampling noise. That gap is large, and it says something structural.

54.5%
I², Family A — surface & behavioural
84.8%
I², Family B — covert & consciential
The variation within the covert family is currently large enough to swamp the variation between families.What the heterogeneity is actually telling us

What drives that heterogeneity. Inside Family B, effect sizes range from h=0.241 (EEGNet, five imagined Spanish words, n=24) to h=1.540 (LSTM with wavelet scattering, four imagined commands, n=4). That spread isn't small variation around one method — it's a field still disagreeing with itself on basics:

  • Vocabulary size, from 4 to 13 classes
  • Electrode count, from a single channel to a full EEG cap
  • Populations and sample sizes that vary just as widely
  • Not one of the five studies tested a single person with aphasia

Family A, by contrast, has mostly converged on a shared pipeline shape: ASR or SVM classifiers trained on larger, more standardised clinical datasets.

In other words, the family level comparison averages together a field that is still deciding on its own basic engineering parameters. A non significant difference doesn't mean the two approaches are equally mature.

A moderator analysis by technical factor — channel count, vocabulary size, invasive versus non invasive recording — would likely explain more of that variance than the surface versus covert split did on its own.

A check for publication bias

A high pooled effect size can mean the technique works, or it can mean the studies that failed never got published. The usual way to catch the difference is a funnel plot: effect size against standard error, which should form a symmetric triangle if small studies are published whether they succeed or not.

Asymmetry — small, imprecise studies clustering on the favourable side — is the warning sign.

Reading this honestly. Neither test finds statistically significant asymmetry — but that is not the same as "no publication bias":

  • With k=9 studies, both tests are severely underpowered. The conventional threshold for trusting a funnel plot at all is k=10, and Egger's test specifically is known to have low power below k=20.
  • The correct reading is "not enough studies to tell," the same honest limit this project already states for its heterogeneity analysis.
  • The two visibly low, imprecise Family B points on the plot's left side (EEGNet, single-channel EEG) are exactly what a biased literature would hide. Their presence is mildly reassuring, but a field this small could look symmetric or asymmetric by chance either way.

Answering the question that started this direction

The original framing — whether AI can imitate and understand the conscious events of aphasia — now has a more precise answer than a simple yes or no.

On average, AI systems attempting to decode inner speech directly from neural signal reach accuracy statistically indistinguishable from AI systems working on overt behaviour.

But that average hides a field that hasn't yet settled on what a reliable covert decoding setup even looks like, and one that hasn't yet been tested on a single person with aphasia in this sample.

The clinical population this entire project is about is, so far, absent from exactly the literature meant to reach it most directly.Section 7, on the sample behind Family B

What would settle this properly. Three concrete next steps, in order of likely impact.

  1. A moderator meta-regression using channel count and vocabulary size as covariates, to test whether the heterogeneity in Family B collapses once technical setup is controlled for.
  2. Expanding Family B specifically with studies run on people with aphasia rather than healthy volunteers, the population this project is actually about.
  3. Full text extraction for the highest profile covert decoding studies (Kunz et al. 2025, Bhadra et al. 2025), currently excluded from pooling because open vocabulary decoding has no directly comparable chance baseline.
